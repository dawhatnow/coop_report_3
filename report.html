00 / abstract

What this report is

I spent my first co-op work term as a Data Governance & Quality co-op at Equitable Life of Canada, from May to August 2026. I came in expecting to write code. I spent most of the term writing models, documentation, and explanations instead — and that turned out to be the more useful lesson.

If you read only one thing here, read the reflection at the end. The projects are real and I'm proud of them, but the thing I'll carry forward is a habit I didn't have in May: not stopping at a number that looks correct.

01 / employer

Equitable Life of Canada

Equitable Life is one of Canada's largest mutual life insurance companies. Being a mutual company means it has no external shareholders — it's owned by its participating policyholders, which shapes how decisions get weighed internally in a way I hadn't encountered before.

My team sat inside Data & Analytics. Data governance is the discipline of making an organization's data trustworthy: defining what a term officially means, tracking who owns which dataset, measuring quality, and giving people a way to find and understand data without having to know a specific person who happens to remember how a table works. In computing terms it lives at the intersection of data modelling, reporting infrastructure, and information architecture — and a surprising amount of it is a communication problem wearing a technical costume.

02 / goals

What I set out to learn

I wrote these in my first two weeks. I've marked each one honestly, including the one I only partly got to.

  • Met

    Get genuinely fluent in Power BI and dimensional modelling

    I wanted a real reporting tool on my resume rather than a tutorial-level familiarity. By August I was building models, writing DAX measures, and — more importantly — able to explain why a model was structured the way it was.

  • Met

    Contribute to a governance deliverable that people actually use

    I wanted to ship something with an audience, not a sandbox exercise. The Data Governance report went to stakeholders who make decisions from it, which meant my errors had consequences — the most useful pressure I've worked under so far.

  • Met

    Improve technical communication by teaching what I learned

    I set out to present at least once to people more senior than me. I ended up writing learning material on star schemas for our internal platform and presenting to the Data & Analytics community of practice — an audience that already knew the topic better than I did, which is exactly why it was worth doing.

  • Met

    Understand how client experience data travels end to end

    Supporting the Voice of the Client work let me follow data from the contact centre platform all the way to a number in front of a decision-maker, and see how many judgement calls sit in between.

  • Partly met

    Move from reporting the data to interrogating it

    This is the honest one. I got good at answering "what does this show?" and I was slower to ask "why does it show that, and should we trust it?" I caught the habit late in the term and it became the piece of feedback I most want to act on. Naming it here is deliberate — it's the goal I'm carrying into my next work term rather than quietly closing.

03 / the work

What I actually did

Three threads ran through the term. The first two were assigned; the third started as something to do during a slow week and ended up being the work I'm proudest of.

Thread 01

Data governance reporting

Supported the build of a Power BI dashboard tracking data governance and quality metrics, so that stakeholders could see the state of our data without someone assembling it by hand each cycle. Most of the difficulty wasn't the visuals — it was agreeing on what each metric meant before anything could be counted.

04 / reflection

What changed in how I think

The most valuable feedback I got had nothing to do with tooling. It was that I needed to develop more curiosity about the why behind the data — to treat a number as the start of a question rather than the end of a task.

A dashboard that renders correctly and a dashboard that's right are two different deliverables, and only one of them shows up in the build.

Looking back, I could see the pattern. When a metric came back lower than expected, my instinct was to check my measure for bugs. The better instinct — the one the people around me had — was to ask whether the underlying process changed, whether a source system started recording something differently, whether the definition everyone was using had quietly drifted. Correct code over a misunderstood input produces a confident wrong answer, and those are the expensive kind.

The second thing I'm working on is interpersonal communication. I found presenting to a room easier than the smaller, more frequent thing: proactively pulling someone aside early with a half-formed question. I defaulted to figuring it out alone longer than I should have, which is a habit that reads as self-sufficiency and is often just slower.

The unexpected takeaway is how much of technical work is definitional. Weeks of the term went into getting people to agree on what a word meant before any of it could be measured. I came in thinking data governance was a constraint on engineering. I left thinking it's the thing that makes engineering output trustworthy enough to act on.

05 / conclusion

Where this leaves me

If you took one thing from this page, I'd want it to be this: I spent four months learning that the hard part of data work isn't building the model, it's knowing what the model is claiming and whether that claim holds. I can build a dimensional model and a Power BI report end to end. I can teach the concept to people who already know it. And I now know precisely which instinct I'm missing and what it costs — which is a better position than finishing a work term with nothing but a list of things that went well.

Next term I want to work somewhere the data volume forces the question earlier, and I want to be the person who asks it first.
